Unit 1 Overview — What to Expect

Understand how HCI bridges human capabilities and machine interfaces; distinguish UI from UX; apply empathy maps, mental models, semantic HTML, WCAG 2.2 POUR accessibility, and usability/UX goals.

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish UI from UX and analyze Affordance, the Gulf of Execution, and the Gulf of Evaluation.
  • Build Empathy Maps, compare qualitative vs. quantitative research, and apply Semantic HTML.
  • Evaluate digital interfaces against WCAG 2.2 POUR principles (4.5:1 contrast, focus rings, captions/transcripts).
  • Differentiate objective Usability Goals from subjective UX Goals.

Lesson 1.1: Foundations of HCI, UX, and UI Design

Jump to Quiz 1.1 ↓
Key Reminder: Affordance Warning: Never design controls whose visual appearance contradicts their physical or digital action (like a 'Norman Door' pull handle on a push-only door).

What Is HCI? Closing Don Norman's Two Gulfs

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) blends Computer Science, Cognitive Psychology, and Behavioral Science to design technology around human needs. Designers work to close two gaps: the Gulf of Execution (gap between user intention and available interface actions) and the Gulf of Evaluation (gap between system state and clear feedback).

Five Interface Types & UI vs. UX

UI is the visible/tactile surface (buttons, icons, layout), whereas UX is the entire end-to-end journey, efficiency, and emotional satisfaction. Interface paradigms include GUI (visual icons), CLI (typed commands like mkdir), VUI (voice assistants), PUI (physical knobs/dials), and Menu-Driven UI (ATMs).

Empathy Maps, Research Lenses & Mental Models

A four-quadrant Empathy Map captures what users Say (quotes), Think (unspoken worries), Do (actions), and Feel (emotions). Effective teams pair Quantitative research ("the what": analytics, A/B tests, 65% cart drop-off) with Qualitative research ("the why": 5–12 user interviews).

Under Jakob's Law, users expect your app to work like other apps they already know (Mental Models). Using semantic HTML tags (<header>, <nav>, <main>, <button>, <label>) aligns page structure with assistive screen readers.

Lesson 1.3: Accessibility and Inclusiveness (WCAG 2.2)

Jump to Quiz 1.3 ↓
Key Reminder: Color-Alone Warning: Never signal form errors or status using red/green color alone—pair color with text, icons, or patterns.

Social Model of Disability & WCAG 2.2 POUR Principles

Under the social model of disability, barriers reside in poorly designed interfaces rather than in the user. WCAG 2.2 Level AA rests on four principles:

  • Perceivable: Alt text for images, captions/transcripts for video, and at least 4.5:1 color contrast for normal text.
  • Operable: Full keyboard navigation with visible focus rings (3:1 contrast) and no flashing > 3 times/sec.
  • Understandable: Plain-language error recovery, persistent labels, and consistent navigation.
  • Robust: Clean semantic markup parsed reliably by screen readers (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver).

Lesson 1.4: Usability and User Experience Goals

Jump to Quiz 1.4 ↓
Key Reminder: Safety vs. Speed Warning: Removing confirmation dialogs on destructive actions (like 'Delete Account') increases clicks/sec speed but violates Usability Safety.

Objective Usability vs. Subjective UX

Usability Goals measure objective task performance: Effectiveness (Task Success Rate), Efficiency (Time on Task), Safety (error prevention & undo), Utility (right tools for the job), and Learnability (ease for first-time users). UX Goals capture subjective emotion: Satisfying, Fun/Engaging, Rewarding, and Motivating—benchmarked via the 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) (0–100 score).
